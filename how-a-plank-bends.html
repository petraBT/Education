How much does a plank bend?

Put a person on the end of a plank sticking out of a wall. It sags. This page builds up, one idea at a time, how an engineer predicts by how much.

Tip sag:   (2 m long, 30 cm wide, bending drawn 5× larger than real)

The deflection y(x) is how far the plank has dropped below its unloaded position (the dashed line) at a distance x from the wall. The sag is just the deflection at the tip, y(L).

Try it with a ruler. Hold a ruler flat on a desk with half of it hanging over the edge and press the free end. Now turn it on its edge and press again. Same ruler, same material, same push. Why is one so much stiffer?

1Cut the plank in your mind

Imagine sawing through the plank somewhere along its length. The outer piece, with the person on it, would fall. So at every point, the part of the plank nearer the wall must be holding the outer part in place. It does two jobs.

It holds the outer piece up, with a force equal to the load. That’s the shear force, and here it’s the same everywhere: V = P.

It also stops the outer piece from rotating. The load acts like a person pushing on a wrench: the farther away it is, the stronger the twist. That twist is the bending moment.

M = P × (distance from the cut to the load)

Slide the cut toward the wall and the lever arm grows, so the moment grows. At the wall it’s largest, P × L. At the tip it’s zero. This is why diving boards and tree branches break near where they’re attached, not at the end.

2Moment makes the plank curve

A bending moment doesn’t move the plank all at once. It makes each little piece of the plank curve a little. Big moment, sharp curve. Small moment, gentle curve. Zero moment, straight.

How sharply a piece curves also depends on how stiff the plank is. Engineers lump that stiffness into one number, EI, the flexural rigidity:

how sharply it curves = M / EI

E is the material: steel is about 20 times stiffer than wood. I is the shape of the cross-section. For a rectangle of width b and thickness h, I = bh³/12. That cube on h is the answer to the ruler puzzle: turning the ruler on its edge makes it about 30 times thicker in the bending direction.

3Add up the little bends

Here is the key idea. Chop the plank into pieces. Give each piece a small kink, sized by the bending moment at that spot. Start at the wall, where the plank is fixed flat, and lay the pieces end to end. Out comes the shape of the plank.

Big dots mark big kinks. Notice they’re largest near the wall, where the moment is largest, and they shrink to nothing at the tip. With a handful of pieces the shape is rough. With more pieces, it settles onto a smooth curve.

Adding up lots of small changes is exactly what integration does. The kinks change the slope of the plank, and the slope changes its height. Two additions, so two integrations.

4Now the equation

Write y for how far the plank has dropped at a distance x from the wall. “How sharply it curves” is the second derivative, y″, so step 2 becomes

EI · y″ = M = P(L − x)

Integrate once to get the slope, then again to get the height. Each integration brings a constant, and the wall pins both of them down: at the wall the plank doesn’t drop and doesn’t tilt, so both constants are zero. The result is

y = P x² (3L − x) / 6EI

At the tip, where x = L:

sag = PL³ / 3EI

(Measuring y downward keeps the signs friendly. Textbooks often measure up, which adds minus signs but changes nothing physical.)

6Check yourself

A shelf sags too much. Is it better to make it twice as thick or half as long?

Both help by the same factor: halving L divides the sag by 2³ = 8, and doubling h divides it by 2³ = 8. Halving the length also halves the moment at the wall, which reduces the stress there.

Where is the plank most likely to crack, and why?

At the wall. That’s where the lever arm to the load is longest, so the bending moment is largest.

Why is the first integration constant zero?

It equals the slope at the wall. The plank is built in, so it leaves the wall perfectly flat: slope zero.

Do you need the shear force to find the sag?

Not for this method. You only need the bending moment, the stiffness EI, and what happens at the wall. The shear force is the rate at which the moment changes along the plank, which is useful when you start from the loads instead.
